## 2026-08-30: Deleted the job instead of fixing the bug inside it
**Decided.** A weekly check of mine had been firing a false alert, and I had about twenty minutes of debugging queued up to go fix it. Before I started I looked at what the check was actually reading, and the underlying data had gone twelve days stale, which meant the alert was firing on rows that were already dead. Asking whether a job still deserves to exist is cheaper than debugging it, and it has to come first, or you spend the afternoon carefully repairing something you should have thrown away.

## 2026-08-29: Found four agents told to do a thing they had no tool for
**Learned.** I pulled 8 self-audits, 2 post-mortems, a timing log and the agent definitions into one diagnostic of my job-application pipeline, and two findings reframed the whole set. The pass rate I had been quoting myself, 9 of 10 runs inside the time target, was really 10 of 10 over it: the one passing row passed only because I edited a file mid-run and moved the clock. The larger finding is that all four of my review agents carry a written instruction to append their verdict to a shared log, and not one of them has the tool that would let them do it, which is the mechanism behind both times a reviewer verdict went missing. I implemented 8 of the 9 recommendations the same day, replaced the time target with a real start-time stamp, and added two checks to the pre-submission verifier, sweeping them against real application folders before calling it done. Writing an instruction into a spec does not grant the thing reading it the ability to carry that instruction out, and an agent that cannot comply will fail quietly long before it thinks to tell you.

## 2026-08-27: Traced two dark days to a login screen
**Learned.** Two kernel panics on consecutive nights rebooted my laptop to the disk-encryption unlock screen, and my entire automation stack runs as a user-level scheduled agent that does not exist until somebody logs in, so the 3 AM run never fired on either day. Nothing told me, because the alerting lives inside the script that did not run, and the watchdog I built to catch exactly this reported all green straight through: it was counting a rate, three runs in the last seven days, rather than watching for silence. I shipped two patches the same night, one that fires the runner on login behind a guard checking whether the day's run already completed, and one that emails a completion heartbeat the watchdog now measures against a two-day silence threshold, then proved the first live at 21:14 by watching it see a finished run and no-op without starting a job. A worksheet from a month earlier had already named this exact gap and deferred it as a separate build, and that deferral is what the two dark days actually cost. A scheduler that needs a human logged in has a person in the loop whether or not you designed one in.

## 2026-08-26: Taught my job filter to read the hard requirements

## 2026-08-20: Overruled my own advisors on where the automation should live
**Decided.** Three of my agents debated for three rounds where my scheduled jobs should run, and all three landed on the same answer, buy a dedicated machine and keep everything local, because every job reads files that exist only on my laptop. I disagreed: put the state in a private repository first and the filesystem objection goes away. Rather than argue it further I built the pilot the same day, two repositories with no secrets in them plus one scheduled cloud run, and verified the parts that actually matter inside the environment itself, clone, runtime, a live mail connector against my real inbox, deduplication across 473 message ids, and a commit back to the repository, all passing. The pilot then found the one thing that would have broken a real cutover: the cloud sandbox has no outbound web access at all, so any job that reads the open web has to stay where it is. A small version earns its keep by surfacing the constraint you never thought to ask about, and that constraint only ever shows up in the environment.

## 2026-08-11: Built the health check outside the system it watches
**Shipped.** Eight days earlier an OAuth revocation took down five of my automations plus all three alert paths that were supposed to tell me, because every one of them authenticated through the same grant. The fix is an unauthenticated probe that runs as the very first step of my morning job runner, ahead of anything that needs a token: it hits each script's public endpoint, writes an on-disk sentinel, fires a desktop notification, and clears itself on the next clean run. I verified it against eight cases, including the one that actually matters, where a revoked script returns HTTP 200 with a body reading "Access Denied" and a naive status-code check calls that healthy. It caught a real failure 19 hours after I installed it. A monitor is worth only what it can still detect once the thing it watches is broken, so build it out of fewer moving parts than the system it guards.

## 2026-08-04: Chose a slower pipeline over a faster wrong answer
**Decided.** My job-application workflow was running 26 minutes against a 5-minute target, so I ran a postmortem on where the time actually goes. Most of the proposed speedups held up: a cheaper model for the first draft, parallel fix rounds, a hard cap of one revision loop. I rejected one. Surfacing drafts the moment the automated checks pass, and letting the independent review land as a trailing follow-up, would have put a fabricated job title in front of me formatted as my own resume, because the mechanical checks validate length and keywords but never titles. Speed work stays worth doing right up until it moves a correctness check behind the thing that check exists to protect.

## 2026-08-03: My monitoring failed at the exact moment I needed it
**Learned.** Google revoked the OAuth grant on every Apps Script project on my account overnight and five automations went dark at once. The purpose-built watchdog I wrote to catch exactly this class of failure was one of the five, because it shipped its alerts through the same authorization it was supposed to be watching. So did the dead-man's switch backstopping the watchdog. So did the per-job failure alerts. Three independent safety nets, one shared dependency, three silent days before I noticed on my own. The lesson cost me a long weekend of automation: a monitor that lives inside the failure domain it watches isn't monitoring anything.
